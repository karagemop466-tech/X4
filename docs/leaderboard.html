Leaderboard & Rewards

A competition that rewards top-performing strategies — but scores evidence, not profit. A leaderboard that ranks profit ranks variance, and the winner is whoever got lucky.

Status: no entries. No strategy has passed the minimum-sample gate (G1), because there is no data to score against. Everything below is the design, not a live competition.

Current standings

Divisions

Strategies are promoted and demoted on gate results, not on profit. A strategy cannot reach a staked division without passing the out-of-sample gate.

Scoring

Raw profit is weighted at 0%. That is deliberate, and it is the single most important design decision on this page.

Rewards

Undefined. No prize, no funding source and no eligibility terms have been approved. That is why every reward cell below is blank and marked unverified — these are business decisions (Q-20, Q-21), not facts this project can supply.

Anti-luck rules

  1. Minimum sample before ranking. Below the threshold a strategy is displayed as "provisionally tracked, unranked" rather than ranked. (Q-16)
  2. Submission cap plus a multiplicity correction. Without this, the best of N submitted strategies looks skilled under pure chance, and the leaderboard systematically rewards the luckiest entrant. (Q-22, S-18)
  3. Out-of-sample required for promotion. In-sample edge is nearly free to manufacture. (G4)
  4. Profit excluded from scoring. Even after 1,000 bets there was roughly a 1-in-10 chance that luck alone produced a 2% profit where the expectation was a 2% loss.
  5. Capacity is a cap, not a bonus. A strategy that cannot be executed at the size it assumes is capped at what it can actually do. (G7)
  6. An award for well-documented kills. Rewarding a clear write-up of why a strategy was killed by a gate removes the incentive to keep a failing strategy alive until it happens to look good.

Guard against our own judgement

One row in the anti-luck table on the Validation Gates page is not a statistic at all: it is a reminder. Peer-reviewed survey evidence finds participants in both daily fantasy and sports betting rate their own activity as more skill than luck — means of 6.10 and 5.90 on an 11-point scale where 10 is pure skill — and that a false perception of control is positively related to gambling problems. Our own conviction that a strategy is good is evidence of nothing.
